Venezia–Napoli: two teams that struggle on the counter.

Venezia are bottom on zero points, but they shoot, have players who create and press when they can. Napoli come from Villarreal with three counter-attacks conceded. Two teams that leave space behind: the game may depend on who leaves more.

Venezia–Napoli, on Saturday at 15:00 at the Penzo stadium, is matchday 7 of Serie A. Venezia are bottom on zero points, with six defeats in six games, and changed coach a few days ago: Stroppa out, Davide Nicola in, making his debut against Napoli.

Napoli come from the defeat at Villarreal, with three goals conceded on counter-attacks. Allegri said in his press conference that they need to “manage the ball better at certain moments of the game”.

On paper it is a game with an obvious favourite. The data say it is less simple than it looks, and that the two teams share the same problem: the space behind them when they lose the ball. To understand it we analysed Venezia’s six games this season.

A new Venezia, for one game

The news comes from the press and is updated to Friday 16 October. The official line-ups arrive an hour before the game.

Venezia sacked Stroppa after the 3-1 at Bergamo, the sixth defeat in six, with two goals conceded in the first six minutes. Nicola, a specialist in rescue jobs, has had very little training time. According to the Gazzetta he should keep Stroppa’s set-up, the 3-5-2 with Yeboah and Adams up front and two selection dilemmas on the wings; Sky speaks of a few changes. Correia, who went off injured at Bergamo, is to be assessed.

In Napoli Anguissa, Alisson and Olivera are back, and Marianucci and Beukema have recovered. Neres is missing, injured again. Allegri has to choose between Lucca and Højlund, and said the double centre-forward “can be a solution”. According to the press De Bruyne could start on the bench.

Data sample. The analysis uses Venezia’s six games (Lecce, Frosinone, Fiorentina, Milan, Lazio and Atalanta) and the eight Napoli games already analysed. These are few games, and all with Stroppa on the bench: the numbers show trends, not certainties.

A Venezia that doesn’t sit back

The first fact is this: Venezia don’t play like a bottom side. They play a 3-5-2, keep the ball a lot (between 60 and 71% of the passes in four games out of six) and shoot plenty: 90 shots in six games, more than their opponents against Lecce, at Frosinone and against Lazio.

The problem is at the other end: they have scored 5 goals. To understand why, it helps to look at who plays.

Three players who tell the story of Venezia

Hainaut, the right wing-back, is the one who has crossed the most: 35 crosses in six games, almost all from open play, but only 6 reached a team-mate. He has still scored two of Venezia’s five goals. Against him, Napoli will have their left side: the press points to Spinazzola.

Yeboah is the striker who invents: 29 dribbles, 11 successful, and 12 key passes, as many as Kike Pérez. Adams is the one who should finish: 21 shots, 16 from inside the box, one goal. Together they have shot 40 times and scored 2.

Kike Pérez, a central midfielder, is the player everything goes through: the most involved in the team and the one who takes the most corners (10 out of 26).

Which Venezia will show up?

Pressing is measured with PPDA: how many passes you let the opponent make before stepping in. The lower the number, the more the team presses.

Against Lecce, Frosinone and Fiorentina, Venezia pressed hard (PPDA between 7 and 8). Against Milan, Lazio and Atalanta much less (between 13 and 17). For Venezia, Napoli are an opponent closer to the second group, but Nicola is not Stroppa: the Gazzetta describes him as a coach with no preconceived ideas about the set-up. No data can say which Venezia we will see.

When they have less of the ball, Venezia don’t have a single habit: against Milan (42% of the passes) they defended at about 38 metres from their own goal, against Lazio (50%) at about 28. One fact is steadier: after the break Venezia rise. The average height of their defensive actions goes from about 31 to about 37 metres: in Florence from 26 to 40, against Milan from 33 to 42.

Rising means leaving more space behind the line, and six of the sixteen goals Venezia conceded came from the 65th minute on.

Not every lost ball is a counter-attack

Of the sixteen goals conceded, six are real counter-attacks: one to nine seconds after the opponent’s ball recovery, with at most two passes. Four came against Milan and Atalanta, two just at Bergamo, in the 3rd and the 29th minute.

The other ten arise differently: three from a set piece or penalty, seven from attacks with settled possession. In Florence the 28th-minute goal starts from a ball recovery, but then the move lasts 59 seconds and 18 passes: it is not a counter-attack, it is a built attack. Against Lazio the goal in the 59th minute comes after 41 seconds and 13 passes.

The picture is therefore more precise than “Venezia concede when they lose the ball”: they suffer counter-attacks mainly against Milan and Atalanta, while against the others they concede from more built-up attacks. Twice they have conceded two goals in two minutes: at Bergamo, in the 3rd and 5th minute, and against Fiorentina, in the 28th and 29th.

Napoli and the ball

Napoli have a clear fact: they have never lost when they had more than half of the passes. They won against Genoa, Bologna and Frosinone and drew at Florence. The four defeats, against Como, Inter, Arsenal and Villarreal, all came below 50%.

Having the ball, though, also means protecting it. Five of the ten goals Napoli have conceded in eight games are real opposition counter-attacks, three of them at Villarreal. Corriere dello Sport suggests going back to the double playmaker, Lobotka and Gilmour in front of the defence, as against Bologna and Fiorentina.

Against a Venezia that rise after the break, Napoli can find space to break. But the first half-hour is another story: if Venezia press high as against Lecce and Frosinone, every wrong pass near your own box is a gift.

Who goes ahead

There is a second thing in common, and it concerns what happens after the first goal. Napoli went ahead first in six games out of eight, but won three of them: at Genoa, against Bologna and against Frosinone. Against Inter they lost 3-2 after being two goals up, at Florence they drew and at Villarreal they lost.

Venezia have never held a lead. In six games they have gone ahead once, against Fiorentina in the 21st minute, and eight minutes later they were already behind. At Frosinone they managed to come back from 0-2 to 2-2, but lost in the 82nd.

The four things to watch on Saturday

Who leaves more space

Venezia–Napoli is not a game without a story, even if the table says otherwise. Venezia are bottom but have players who create and a team that shoots. Napoli have one way to win, the ball, and one way to lose, the counter-attack.

Venezia create but don’t score, Napoli win when they have the ball: the game may depend on who leaves more space behind.
